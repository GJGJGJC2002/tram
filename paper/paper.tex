\section{Abstract}

version1:
Acquiring high-quality, large-scale human motion and scene interaction data is crucial for simulation and embodied intelligence. Recovering global 3D human motion from monocular videos captured by moving cameras is an important pathway toward this goal; however, the tight coupling between camera motion and human motion in image observations makes this problem extremely challenging. Existing methods mainly fall into two paradigms: two-stage approaches that independently estimate camera motion and human motion, thereby disrupting the kinematic properties and scene interaction of the human body; and end-to-end approaches that directly estimate both, yet lack explicit geometric constraints, resulting in inferior performance and error accumulation.

Our method is inspired by the human cognitive process: when observing another person's motion, humans mentally fix a viewpoint, reconstruct a scale-consistent local motion sequence based on scene cues, and then leverage their understanding of motion to localize themselves and reconstruct the surrounding environment. Based on this insight, we propose EIHMR, introducing a novel paradigm for motion-scene reconstruction under moving cameras. The framework transforms human motion---which originally interferes with camera localization---into reliable visual cues through two tightly coupled stages: Scene-Aware Local Human Motion Reconstruction re-projects the motion sequence into a unified viewpoint and performs kinematic and scene-interaction optimization under metric depth constraints to produce geometrically consistent local motion. The refined motion is then re-rendered into the original frames, converting dynamic human regions into structured visual features, which a Motion-aware SLAM module exploits for robust camera estimation. On the EMDB dataset, EIHMR reduces the global trajectory error by approximately 11.5\%, demonstrating its outstanding capability in long-range scene-aligned human motion reconstruction.

\section{Introduction}

Understanding how humans move through the world from visual data is a fundamental problem in computer vision. To fully capture this motion from a video, we need to recover not only the kinematic body poses but also the global trajectory in world coordinates (\cref{fig:overview}). This capability is essential for downstream applications such as human-scene interaction understanding~\cite{Hassan2019,RICH2022}, embodied intelligence~\cite{Embodied2022,EmbodiedScan2024}, and digital human animation~\cite{DigitalHuman2024}. The task becomes particularly challenging when the input is a monocular video captured by a moving camera, where camera motion and human motion are tightly entangled in image observations.

Existing methods broadly follow two paradigms. Two-stage approaches~\cite{TRAM2024,SLAHMR2023,PACE2024} first estimate camera trajectories via SLAM, then independently reconstruct human poses, and subsequently obtain global trajectories through coordinate transformation. However, the SLAM stage typically requires masking dynamic human regions to satisfy the static-scene assumption~\cite{DynaSLAM2018}, when humans dominate the field of view, the available feature points are drastically reduced, degrading camera estimation quality. The independently estimated human poses are further corrupted when projected through inaccurate camera trajectories, producing foot sliding and trajectory drift. End-to-end approaches~\cite{WHAM2024,TRACE2023,GLAMR2022,GVHMR2024} attempt to directly regress global human motion but either rely on motion capture priors with limited generalization capability, or employ autoregressive mechanisms that accumulate errors over time.

The common bottleneck across these approaches is that camera estimation and human estimation typically operate as a unidirectional pipeline, lacking an explicit mechanism for mutual reinforcement, which causes errors in one stage to cascade and amplify. We argue that breaking through this bottleneck requires addressing two key questions: (1) How can geometrically accurate local human motion be obtained despite the coordinate-frame ambiguity introduced by a moving camera? (2) How can refined human motion be leveraged, in an exploitable form, to in turn improve global camera localization?

For the first question, our core observation stems from the limitations of temporal human motion estimation: to maintain cross-frame smoothness, temporal HMR tends to sacrifice geometric consistency with single-frame image observations when the camera trajectory is biased, causing the reconstructed body to progressively deviate from the actual observations in the video. When projected into the world coordinate system, these deviations manifest in more tangible forms: feet that should remain in static contact exhibit sliding and cumulative drift, and limbs penetrate or float above scene surfaces such as the ground and stairs—artifacts that violate physical scene constraints. Humans do not blindly accept visual intuition when interpreting others' motion; instead, they mentally fix a viewing angle and rely on body–scene contact relationships to calibrate and localize the motion sequence. Inspired by this observation, we perform local motion reconstruction in keyframe viewpoints: motion within each window is projected into a unified keyframe viewpoint, thereby eliminating camera motion interference and enabling metric assessment and optimization within a stable reference frame. In this "frozen-camera" reference frame, established techniques such as metric depth estimation and kinematic constraints can be directly applied, yielding reliable local subgraph reconstructions that serve as anchors for subsequent global alignment.

For the second question, we observe that existing methods treat dynamic humans as a source of interference for SLAM and simply mask them out. However, this discards precisely the most information-rich region of the image—the human body typically occupies the central field of view and, in scenes with sparse background texture, may constitute the only reliable source of visual cues. Consider, by contrast, the human visual cognitive process: when observing a scene containing a moving person, we do not blindly ignore the human body but rather leverage our intrinsic kinematic priors—an innate understanding of skeletal structure, joint degrees of freedom, and typical motion patterns—to interpret the moving body as a three-dimensional entity with determinate geometry, thereby aiding the reconstruction of our own pose and the surrounding environment. Motivated by this insight, we re-render the refined human motion back into the original image frames in a geometrically consistent manner, transforming the dynamic human body—which originally interferes with camera localization—into a cooperative partner that provides dense matching cues.

Building on these two insights, we propose EIHMR: a cooperative framework that enables mutual reinforcement between human motion reconstruction and camera trajectory estimation. The framework consists of two stages: Scene-Aware Local Human Motion Reconstruction (SA-LHMR) and Motion-Aware SLAM (MO-SLAM). SA-LHMR constructs a unified reference frame from keyframe viewpoints, projects both single-frame and temporal motion estimates into this shared viewpoint, and performs sliding-window optimization under metric depth and kinematic constraints to produce kinematically consistent and scene-plausible local motion. MO-SLAM renders this local motion back into the original frames in a geometrically consistent manner, synthesizing motion-augmented images that transform human regions from "excluded dynamic interference" into structured cues available for feature matching, thereby improving the robustness of camera estimation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We introduce EIHMR, a cooperative architecture that couples human motion reconstruction with camera trajectory estimation, based on the principle of freezing the viewpoint for local refinement and using motion augmented observations for robust localization.
  \item We propose SA-LHMR, which performs scene aware local motion reconstruction in keyframe viewpoints by enforcing metric depth and kinematic constraints, effectively reducing foot skating and scene penetration artifacts.
  \item We propose MO-SLAM, which converts refined human motion into geometrically consistent features for SLAM through re rendering, improving robustness in human dominated and low texture scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}

\subsection{Pipeline}

Our goal is to recover complete 3D human motion from monocular video $\mathcal{V} = \{I_t\}_{t=0}^{T}$ captured by an in-the-wild moving camera. We decompose the global motion into an $SE(3)$ root trajectory $\{H_t\}_{t=0}^{T}$ in the world coordinate system and a kinematic body motion represented by SMPL pose sequences $\{\Theta_t\}_{t=0}^{T}$ in the camera coordinate system. The global human motion can be obtained by estimating the camera trajectory $\{C_t\}_{t=0}^{T}$ and the human position relative to the camera at each time step $\{T_t\}_{t=0}^{T}$.

Existing methods typically rely on SLAM algorithms to obtain the camera trajectory $\{C_t\}$ and fuse SLAM-provided angular velocity information as auxiliary features with visual features for human motion estimation $\{\Theta_t\} = \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{V}, \{C_t\})$. However, directly incorporating angular velocity as a conditioning feature jointly with visual features for motion perception has not yielded satisfactory motion separation results. When two variables are mutually coupled, an effective strategy is to first fix one variable to characterize the other's variation, and then use the obtained patterns to analyze the fixed variable. We adopt a similar strategy for decoupling camera motion and human motion.

Specifically, we select a set of keyframes $\mathcal{K} = \{k_i\}$ from the video sequence and use each keyframe's viewpoint as the reference coordinate system for local motion reconstruction. For keyframe $k_i$, we project the human motion of neighboring frames within its sliding window $\mathcal{W}_i$ into the keyframe's viewpoint and refine it using kinematic constraints. Formally, we recover redundant human root trajectory sequences $\{H_{ij}^{k}\}$ on the keyframes, where $i$ is the keyframe index, $j$ is the frame offset within the window, and $H_{ij}^{k}$ denotes the human root trajectory at frame offset $j$ from the center, viewed from the camera coordinate system centered at keyframe $k_i$. Together with the corresponding body poses $\{\Theta_{ij}\}$, we obtain the complete local motion sequence from the viewpoint of $k_i$:
%
\begin{equation}
  \{\Theta_{ij}', H_{ij}^{k'}\} = \text{SA-LHMR}\left(I_{k_i}, \{\Theta_{ij}\}, \{H_{ij}^{k}\}\right)
\end{equation}

Since all frames share a unified viewpoint, camera motion is completely eliminated, enabling us to leverage kinematic methods and more mature image-domain techniques---such as metric depth estimation---for scene-aware motion refinement. Specifically, we employ static joint analysis from forward kinematics to correct the root trajectory, and detect scene penetrations via metric depth maps to refine body poses through inverse kinematics. The detailed implementation of SA-LHMR is presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:sa-lhmr}.

After obtaining the refined local motion sequences $\{\Theta_{ij}', H_{ij}^{k'}\}$ in the keyframe viewpoints, we use them to improve SLAM's global localization accuracy. Existing methods such as TRAM propose a Mask-SLAM strategy that excludes human regions via masks to reduce dynamic object interference, i.e., $\{C_t\} = \text{Mask-SLAM}(\{I_t\}, \{M_t\})$. However, in human-centric videos, human motion often occupies the central region of the frame; when performing feature matching between two frames $i$ and $j$, the area covered by $M_i \cup M_j$ is excluded from matching, and the irregular mask shapes also severely degrade SLAM's feature extraction quality. Furthermore, in low-texture regions, SLAM also struggles to obtain reliable matching cues.

Based on these observations, we propose MO-SLAM (Motion-aware SLAM). This module leverages the local motion sequences obtained in the SA-LHMR stage to re-render the refined SMPL parameters into 2D images, generating motion-augmented images $\{\tilde{I}_t\}$ that transform the moving human body into a multi-view geometrically consistent static mesh sequence:
%
\begin{equation}
  \{C_t\} = \text{MO-SLAM}\left(\{\tilde{I}_t\}, \{M_t\}\right)
\end{equation}

These human meshes with cross-frame consistent geometric structure provide SLAM with kinematically aware dense visual features, significantly improving the quality of feature matching and the robustness of camera estimation, especially in low-texture regions and scenarios with large-area human occlusion. The detailed implementation of MO-SLAM is presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:mo-slam}.

\subsection{SA-LHMR}
\label{sec:sa-lhmr}

When the motion sequence is re-projected into the unified viewpoint of the keyframe, many non-physical artifacts in the motion estimation become clearly discernible: the human model lacks proper contact with the scene ground, foot joints that should remain stationary exhibit kinematically implausible skating displacements, and limbs penetrate scene surfaces such as stairs and floors. These non-physical motion artifacts arise from two sources: first, estimation errors in the initial SLAM-provided camera parameters; second, the temporally-aware HPE network sacrificing geometric consistency with image observations to maintain temporal smoothness under erroneous camera conditioning. Notably, these errors can be explicitly measured and corrected through forward kinematics and scene depth, yet are difficult for end-to-end deep networks to learn effectively.

Inspired by the human motion cognition process---when estimating another person's motion trajectory, humans do not rely solely on reflexive intuition but mentally reconstruct the motion sequence, paying particular attention to key body-scene interaction sites (such as foot-ground contact locations) for precise motion localization---we propose a two-stage motion refinement strategy. In the first stage, we correct the root trajectory of the motion sequence based on static joint analysis from forward kinematics. In the second stage, we detect scene-penetrating contact points using metric depth maps and optimize body poses via inverse kinematics to eliminate penetrations. Crucially, since all frames under the unified viewpoint share a single depth map reference, we can constrain multiple contact points across multiple frames within the entire window using a single depth estimate, forming a powerful spatiotemporal consistency constraint.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Root Trajectory Correction via Forward Kinematics.}
We traverse the local sequence under keyframe $k_i$ in a sliding-window manner. For a window $\mathcal{W}_c = [c{-}W, c{+}W]$ of size $2W{+}1$ anchored at center frame $c$, we first compute the world-coordinate joint positions for each frame within the window via forward kinematics (FK):
%
\begin{equation}
  J_{ij} = \text{FK}(\Theta_{ij}, \boldsymbol{\beta}, H_{ij}^{k}), \quad j \in \mathcal{W}_c
\end{equation}

The HPE network simultaneously outputs per-frame static confidence scores $\mathbf{s}_t \in [0,1]^6$ (corresponding to the left/right ankles, toes, and wrists), which are used to identify joints that should maintain static contact with the scene. For joints identified as static, we compute their inter-frame displacements and use them as correction signals for the root trajectory: since static joints should not undergo displacement in the world coordinate system, the inverse of their displacement is back-propagated to the root trajectory $H_{ij}^{k}$ to eliminate trajectory deviations introduced by camera estimation errors and HPE temporal smoothing. The correction process is anchored at the window center frame, keeping its root position unchanged and only adjusting the relative displacements of neighboring frames.

For frames in overlapping window regions, the corrected root trajectories contributed by each window are merged via normalized Gaussian weights based on the frame-to-center distance:
%
\begin{equation}
  H_{j}^{k'} = \frac{\sum_{i} w_{ij} \cdot H_{ij}^{k'}}{\sum_{i} w_{ij}}, \quad w_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(j - c_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\end{equation}
%
where $c_i$ is the center frame index of the $i$-th window and $\sigma$ controls the Gaussian decay bandwidth, ensuring smooth transitions across overlapping regions.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Contact-Aware Inverse Kinematics via Scene Depth.}
After root trajectory correction, the motion sequence may still contain non-physical phenomena where limbs penetrate scene surfaces---for example, feet passing through stair steps or the ground plane. We leverage the metric depth map $D_c = \text{Metric3D}(I_{k_i})$ estimated from the keyframe viewpoint to detect and correct these penetrations.

First, we perform window-local scale alignment. Since scale discrepancies may exist between the SLAM-estimated camera coordinate system and the metric depth map, we use all 22 joints of the center frame as sample points to compute a scale factor between the SMPL joint depths $z_j^{\text{smpl}}$ and the depth map samples $z_j^{\text{scene}}$:
%
\begin{equation}
  \alpha = \operatorname{median}\!\left(\frac{z_j^{\text{scene}}}{z_j^{\text{smpl}}}\right), \quad j = 1, \ldots, 22
\end{equation}

Subsequently, for each static foot joint in every frame of the window, we check whether it penetrates the scene surface: the joint is projected into the center frame's camera coordinate system, and its scale-aligned depth $z_j^{\text{smpl}} \cdot \alpha$ is compared against the depth map value $z_j^{\text{scene}}$. If $z_j^{\text{smpl}} \cdot \alpha > z_j^{\text{scene}} + \epsilon$ (where $\epsilon$ is the penetration tolerance), the joint is deemed to be penetrating. For each penetrating joint, we obtain its target position in world coordinates via depth map back-projection:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_j^{\text{target}} = R_{cw}^{\top} \left(\frac{z_j^{\text{scene}}}{\alpha} \cdot K^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} u_j \\ v_j \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} - \mathbf{t}_{cw}\right)
\end{equation}
%
where $(u_j, v_j)$ are the joint's pixel coordinates. Finally, we optimize the pose increments $\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}$ along the leg kinematic chains (hip $\to$ knee $\to$ ankle $\to$ toe) for all frames within the window, minimizing the distance from contact joints to their target positions:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{contact}} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \rho\!\left(\left\|\text{FK}(\Theta + \delta\boldsymbol{\theta})_j - \mathbf{p}_j^{\text{target}}\right\|_2\right)
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of all penetrating static joints and $\rho(\cdot)$ is the Geman--McClure robust kernel. The optimization simultaneously enforces pose regularization $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \|\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_2^2$ and a temporal smoothness constraint $\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}}$ to ensure that the corrected poses are physically plausible and temporally coherent. Analogous to the root trajectory correction, the optimized body poses $\Theta_{ij}'$ from each window are merged via Gaussian distance weighting in overlapping regions to produce a globally consistent pose sequence.

\subsection{MO-SLAM}
\label{sec:mo-slam}

With geometrically consistent local motion from SA-LHMR, we now leverage it to improve camera trajectory estimation. Traditional SLAM systems~\cite{ORBSLAM3,DROIDSLAM2021,DPVO2023} assume a static scene, requiring dynamic objects to be masked or down-weighted and thereby discarding potentially useful information. Recent feed-forward 3D reconstruction methods~\cite{DUSt3R2024,MonST3R2024,MASt3RSLAM2025} handle dynamic scenes implicitly but do not model human motion structure. As shown in Stage~III of \cref{fig:overview}, the core idea of MO-SLAM is to render the refined motion back into the video frames so that dynamic human regions, which normally degrade SLAM, instead provide structured geometric cues for inter-frame feature matching.

To construct the motion-augmented images, we first derive the world-to-camera (w2c) transformation from the dual outputs of PromptHMR. Let $R_c, \pi_c$ and $R_w, \pi_w$ denote rotation and translation in the camera and world frames, respectively. The w2c transformation is given by:
\begin{equation}
    R_{\text{w2c}} = R_c \cdot R_w^\top, \quad t_{\text{w2c}} = \pi_c + \Delta - R_{\text{w2c}} \cdot (\pi_w + \Delta),
    \label{eq:w2c}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta$ is a constant offset. For uniformly sampled rendering frames $i$ (every 5 frames), SMPL meshes from three adjacent timesteps $\{i{-}5, i, i{+}5\}$ are projected onto the image plane of frame $i$ and rendered as solid overlays, with different timesteps distinguished by colors sampled via the golden-angle HSV scheme. In the resulting images, dynamic human regions are transformed into structured visual features that encode precise spatiotemporal geometry.

We then feed these motion-augmented images into DROID-SLAM with three targeted modifications. First, rendered frame indices are injected as forced keyframes by modifying the MotionFilter to bypass its optical-flow-based keyframe selection criterion and directly admit frames from the forced set; the Frontend is further prevented from removing any keyframes (\texttt{keyframe\_thresh=0.0}), ensuring that all motion-augmented frames participate in bundle adjustment. Second, we introduce an adaptive image replacement strategy: at each keyframe we compute the grayscale Laplacian variance as a texture score, and when this score falls below a threshold $\tau$---indicating sparse background features---the rendered image replaces the original to provide additional structural constraints, while the original masked image is retained otherwise, allowing MO-SLAM to adapt to varying scene complexity. Third, even with rendered images, we apply human-aware correlation weighting by using human masks to down-weight human regions in DROID-SLAM's correlation computation; although the rendered SMPL meshes provide correct geometric contours, their surface appearance differs from the real image, making high pixel-level matching weights inappropriate. This design exploits motion information at the geometry level while suppressing it at the pixel level.

The camera trajectories $\{G_t\}$ from MO-SLAM are combined with the local pose estimates to recover global motion:
